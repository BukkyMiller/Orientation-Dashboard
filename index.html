Survey Analysis · Studio Results Export · July 21, 2026

New-student orientation is landing well — length, AI overload, and a save-progress bug are the drags

3,115 responses · collected July 16–21, 2026 · WGU online orientation · 13 questions (7 rating scales, 1 multi-select, 5 open-text; ~5,770 substantive written answers analyzed)

Students rate the orientation 4.3 / 5 overall, and 9 in 10 leave feeling prepared, supported, and clear on the content. The self-paced format is the single most-praised design choice. The weak spots are just as consistent: the experience runs long and repetitive (its lowest-scoring item and its most common written complaint), the AI content and AI grader polarize, experienced students want a shorter track — and a cluster of progress-saving bugs is generating the angriest feedback and blocking a small number of students from finishing at all.

Six findings that matter

Finding 1 · Length

Length is the one thing dragging the score — and the time estimate makes it worse

Length is the lowest-scoring item (74% agreement) and the most common written complaint (226+ mentions). It's also the item most correlated with the overall rating (r = 0.51). The 203 students who disagreed on length rate the orientation 3.53 vs the 4.34 average. A recurring aggravator: students report being told to budget 2–2.5 hours and spending 5–7. The named culprits are consistent — Module 3's AI sections (three submodules "that should be one"), Module 2's learning-profile work, and near-duplicate content across Modules 4 and 5.

"I was told to set aside 2-2.5 hours to complete orientation, and I think it took me around 5 hours, and that was with speed reading."
Finding 2 · AI

AI is the most polarizing design choice in the product

The same feature earns love and fury. 91+ students name the instant AI feedback as their favorite thing — it "helped me think more critically… letting me iterate." But 143+ complaints target the AI experience: Module 3 over-weights AI content (77 name it directly), the grader enforces hidden criteria ("it referenced a rubric that was not present in the question"), resubmitting wipes the previous answer, and a vocal group objects to being forced to use AI at all — some on ethical grounds, with ~10 explicitly requesting an opt-out. Several practice chatbots also simply failed to respond, then the grader penalized students for "not chatting enough."

"For the AI section, you need to allow the fact that people refuse to use AI for school as an answer."
Finding 3 · Content Gap

The biggest unmet need: show me what a real course looks like

In both "what was missing?" (Q8) and "what's still unclear?" (Q12), the dominant substantive theme — roughly one in eight answers — is the same: students finish orientation still unable to picture the day-to-day of an actual course. They ask for a sample course, a mock assessment, a syllabus, a portal walkthrough, a first-week checklist. Related unknowns cluster around proctored assessments (95+ asks: format, retakes, webcam setup) and acceleration mechanics — ironic given self-pacing is WGU's headline promise.

"I'm still not exactly sure how the courses work. I don't know what it will look like when I begin my first course or how I will navigate finding the assignments, assessments, etc."
Finding 4 · Belonging

Belonging is the softest outcome — and it moves with preparedness

Belonging (Q11) has the largest neutral block of any item: 13% of students sit on the fence. It correlates strongly with feeling prepared (r = 0.68), and knowing where to get support is the single strongest correlate of preparedness (r = 0.74) — the support-ecosystem content is doing real work. What builds belonging in the verbatims is specific: alumni story videos (237+ mentions — "not alone despite WGU being online-only") and reflection exercises. A small tail (~1.5%) still worries online learning will be isolating.

Finding 5 · Audience Fit

Experienced students are the unhappiest segment

Only 33 students rated the experience negatively overall — but their verbatims cluster hard: graduate students, degree-holders, and returning professionals who found goal-setting and study-skills content "geared toward people who are brand new to college," even "demeaning." Their consistent ask is a shorter, test-out or fast-track version for students with prior degrees, plus program-specific content ("Please build a separate orientation module for each specific program").

"It would be nice to have two different orientations - a more lengthy one for those who are new to college and a much shorter, more concise version for those who already have college degrees."
Finding 6 · Product Bugs

A cluster of platform bugs is producing the angriest feedback — and blocking completions

Bug reports surface in every question, including "what did you enjoy most." The most serious: progress and typed answers lost on logout or device switch (38+ explicit reports; the mobile app doesn't sync to the web), forcing students to redo modules two to four times. Several report being unable to pass orientation at all — with downstream enrollment effects ("told simultaneously complete and not started by IT vs my enrollment counselor"). One student named the platform directly: "Wonderful content, the lazuli platform needs a lot of improvement."

Recommendations

Ordered by expected impact against effort, from the evidence above — grouped by owner.

Course Design Related Issues
1

Cut 30–40% of runtime: collapse Module 3's AI sections to one, merge Modules 4+5, trim reflections.

Targets the lowest-scoring item and #1 complaint. Then publish an honest time estimate — the 2.5-vs-6-hour gap is a trust problem, not just a length problem.

2

Add a "your first course" sample module.

A mock course with a practice assessment, portal walkthrough, and first-week checklist answers the single biggest ask across Q8 and Q12 — and would convert the 6% preparedness fence-sitters.

3

Offer a fast track for experienced students.

A test-out or condensed path for degree-holders defuses the most negative segment; program-specific inserts (and fixing the School of Business placeholder) address the "too generic" thread.

4

Make the AI grader show its rubric — and add an opt-out path.

State the criteria ("give 2–3 examples") in the prompt, accept "I choose not to use AI" as a valid answer, and never penalize students for a chatbot that failed to respond.

Method & caveats

Data. 3,115 survey submissions exported July 21, 2026 (responses July 16–21). Rating items answered by 2,847–2,952 respondents; 186 skipped the first section entirely (143 of those still answered the preparedness section — the survey allows partial completion). Daily mean ratings were stable (4.32–4.41), so pooling across days is sound.

Open text. ~5,770 substantive written answers after filtering empties and "no/none/n-a" variants. All were read in full and theme-coded (one coding pass per question), then every headline theme was verified with keyword matching against the raw data — the counts shown are those conservative floors, so true prevalence is somewhat higher. Themes overlap; shares don't sum to 100%.

Caveats. A noticeable minority of long answers appear AI-drafted (near-identical templates; one student pasted a chatbot's suggested answer verbatim), which likely inflates polished themes like "more real-life examples" and deflates nothing critical. Roughly ten junk/test entries ("xxx", "I am testing this form") were ignored. Q1 uses an Awful→Excellent scale, mapped onto the same five-point display as the agreement items in the data table. Correlations are Pearson r on pairwise-complete responses (n ≈ 2,800–2,920); they indicate association, not causation.
